Lah-gage: Travel Itinerary Mobile Application

Have you ever wondered where your friends traveled during their holidays, or asked for tips on what to do in places they’ve explored? I have, and that’s why I designed this travel app that lets you upload, explore, and connect through personalized itineraries!

15 mins read
Back to my work

Summary

Whenever we had the chance to travel around the States, I often found myself asking friends where they were planning to go, where they had been, and what they would recommend. These conversations would turn into long discussions filled with stories, photos of must-see spots, and food recommendations. But more often than not, we’d forget most of it. And if they sent me a bunch of Google Map links, they’d get lost over time as well.

That’s when I started thinking it would be fun to have an app where you can easily showcase the places you visited and the experiences you had while traveling. Sure, it might not be the most groundbreaking product out there, but I wanted to create something simple and useful. So I designed an app where you can keep all your travel memories in one place, and inspire others with your journeys.

I put together a simple storyboard based on what I've experienced, and see if it resonates with you too!

Although my drawing is questionable, but there's no question that this has happened to us before!

Wait a minute, aren't there tons of similar travel applications out there?

I've downloaded more than five travel itinerary apps (i.e. Viator, Wanderlog, Stellar, TripIt, Tripsy) available in the market to explore for myself, but none of them matched my vision. While I don’t doubt they may work for some users, many of them either fall short of expectations or attempt to be too 'versatile and flexible.' For example, adding features like a bill splitter to a travel app makes it overly complicated in my opinion.

My vision for 'Lah-gage' is to create something incredibly simple and straightforward. Users can easily upload their itineraries and share them with the world, allowing others to see what they’ve been up to and incorporate those experiences into their own plans.

Let me introduce you to 'Lah-gage'!

The name 'Lah-gage' is a play on the word 'luggage,' with a local twist. I wanted to give it a bit of Singaporean flair, reflecting the way we pronounce it.

Homepage

The homepage is designed to be engaging, personalised, and exploration-driven to guide users through a seamless journey from curiosity to action.

We often start with a vague desire to "go somewhere" and then we would gradually narrow it down based on time, budget, inspiration. When I crafted this homepage, I wanted it to support this natural flow with these 4 elements:

The use of animated text cycling through country names captures attention immediately. It sets an energetic, adventurous tone to pull uesrs in visually and emotionally. This animation aims to subtly encourage users to think about where they want to go next, but it's to also show that the app is globally oriented.

By showing a scrollable list of the users's upcoming trips with friends, the experience becomes personally relevant right away. Assuming that the user is planning to travel to Singapore, there will be context-aware suggestions based on that trip to offer content tailored to the user's needs. This helps to turn generic inspiration into practical ideas.

The final element of the homepage encourages users to explore beyond their own plans. By showcasing real itineraries, experiences, and content from other travelers, users are exposed to new destinations, ideas and styles of travel they might not have considered.

The homepage layout is deliberately structured to mirror a natural decision-making process. The layered flow ensures useres always have something engaging to do next, be it searching for an itinerary, checking the current trip plans, or thinking about future destinations.

  • The animated country text sparks curiosity
  • The search bar right below it enables instant action
  • Personal trips and suggestions offer tailored next steps
  • The exploration feed keeps the momentum going by showing what else is possible

Create new trip

The "Create new trip" page is designed to provide users with a welcoming starting point for trip planning, while also enabling collaboration and personalisation throughout the journey.

When a user creates a new trip, the app will display a default banner image of a popular or iconic location in the selected country. Imagery is important and serves multiple purposes:

  • Avoiding a cold, blank state: Starting with a blank or empty page can feel uninviting, especially for users creating a trip for the first time. Having the banner image acts as a warm, welcoming visual cue that immediately brings life to the page
  • Setting the mood: The image helps emotionally anchor the user in the experience of that country. It generates excitement and lets them look forward to planning
  • Providing structure: With a pre-built visual context, it reduces decision fatigue at the early stages. Users don't have to worry about "filling in" every detail right away

While a standard image is shown by default, users can replace the banner with their own travel photo from that country at any time. Users can take ownership at any point of time by allowing them to take control and express their memories or vision for the trip. When shared with friends, a personalised image would make the itinerary feel more genuine and relatable.

This feature also encourages users to return to the app post-trip, knowing they can update their itinerary with their own memories and images, effectively turning it in to a shared travel journal.

During the research phase of this project, I evaluated several existing travel apps to identfy features that felt particularly useful and socially engaging. One standout observation was that a few platforms offered shared itinerary planning, allowing multiple users to contribute to the same trip. I found this concept highly aligned with how people actually plan trips in real life, especially among younger travelers.

Planning a trip isn't a solo effort, especially in groups. Whether it's picking destinations, booking acoomodations, or figuring out what to eat or see, each traveler often has their own preferences. So I decided to incorporate this feature on the app.

Create itinerary details: check-in feature

Based on personal experience, I've often struggled to remember or accurately document what I did during a trip. Especially when the days were packs with activities or unplanned adventures. By the end of the trip, I would try to retrace where I went, what I did, or even what time things happened became difficult to remember.

I imagine many other travelers feel the same way. Between spontaneous detours, long travel days, and trying to coordinate plans with friends, it's honestly unrealistic to expect people to stop and fill in their itinerary in real time. Travel is about being present in the moment and not spending time updating an app every few hours.

That's exactly why I designed the Check-In feature.

Users can stay present and enjoy the experience without feeling pressured to document everything right away. This feature respects the rhythm of real life by offering a low-effort way to keep a log of travel moments without interrupting the travel experience. Since check-ins capture real-time location and time, it reflects the trip more authentically especially if its a spontaneous or unplanned activity.

Rather than trying to remember names, locations, and timestamps, the app takes care of all these data. The user only has to fill in the personal bits at their own convenience. When users look back on their trip or share it with others, the itinerary is more complete thanks to these quick check-ins!

Create itinerary details: add item feature

Beyond the quick Check-In feature, I wanted to give users a more detailed way to manually add entries to their itinerary. In my travels, I've often wated to log more than just major landmarks or well-known attractions. Sometimes it's a local food stall, a graffity wall, a hidden photo spot, or even a stay at a unique Airbnb.

So, I designed the Add Item feature to be flexible. Users can add:

  • Activities: Hiking, sightseeing, shopping, etc
  • Food spots: From fine dining to hole-in-the-wall gems
  • Unlisted or custom locations: Street art, quiet viewpoints or unique local finds
  • Lodging: Hotels, hostels, Airbnbs, or traditional stays like ryokans (in Japan)

When designing this feature, I considered many different types of travel details users might want to log. For example, things like transport, budget, weather, events. But I chose to focus on those four abovementioned categories. Here's why:

I want to focus on what travelers actually care about first and avoid over-granularity.

Yes, people do take transport (e.g. trains, buses, rideshares), but logging transport details can feel too tedious and not particularly useful when shared with others. Designing for the 80% use case, I felt that adding more categories would create clutter, and lead to users feeling overwhelmed.

I actually contemplated for a while whether to include the "Do you recommend this place?" segment in the form. I was hesitant at first mainly because:

  • It felt like an extra step that some users might skip
  • I figured that not everyone has a strong opinion. Some places are just okay, not amazing or terrible
  • And let's be real, not everyone wants to update every little thing, especially after a long day of travel

Then I thought more deeply about my own experiences as a traveler, and that shifted my perspective.

I've been to a lot of places that were heavily recommended in travel vlogs, only to arrive and feel totally underwhelmed. Maybe it was crowded, overpriced, or just not worth the hype. And sometimes I am too lazy to check Google reviews for the places I intend to visit.

Travel isn't all about rainbows and butterflies, and it shouldn't have to be. This simple thumbs up/down feature allows future travelers to set realistic expectations and adds trust and honesty to the itinerary.

View trip itinerary: state-based tabs for multi-destination itineraries

When viewing a trip itinerary, especially for longer journey like a 7-10 day trip, the list of activities, food stops, and lodging entries can quickly grow overwhelming. In many cases, especially in countries like Japan, South Korea, or the United States, travelers often visit multiple cities or states within one trip.

The problem I noticed

From personal experience, I realised that:

  • A single itinerary can contain dozen of entries
  • Travelers often move between different regions in a country (e.g. Tokyo -> Kyoto -> Osaka, or LA -> Seattle -> San Francisco)
  • If someone is only interested in seeing activities in one specific area, they're forced to scroll endlessly to find what they need

This makes the viewing experience less efficient, especially when users revisit their own itinerary to share it with friend or use it for planning reference.

So, I thought of a dynamic state-based tab

To improve navigation and make the experience more intuiative, I added a tab system at the top of the itinerary viewing page.

  • The app automatically detects location data from each entry and groups acitivites by state, province, or region
  • Each area becomes a clickable tab at the top of the itinerary page
  • Users can jump directly to the section of the itinerary for that specific region

For example:

  • A trip to Japan might have tabs for Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka
  • A U.S. trip could show California, Nevada, Arizona
  • In countries like Singapore (where there are no states), this tab structure is simply not shown, keeping the design clean and relevant

Exploration page: dynamic discovery, personalised to the traveler

One of the most exciting part of travel is the discovery, randomly stumbling upon places you hadn't considered or even heard of before. With that in mind, I designed the Explore page to spark spontaneous inspiration while still tailoring the content to user preferences.

When users open the Explore tab, they're shown 3 randomly selected countries. These countries refresh each time the Explore tab is accessed.

Why randomize it?

From my point of view, that's the whole point of exploration. The goal is to simulate the feeling of stumbing across something new, the same way you might discover a destination by scrolling through Instagram or YouTube rabbit hole.

At the end of each country section, there's a simple "Explore more of these" button. Clicking it takes the user to a filtered search results page where it only shows itineraries from that country.

As a traveler myself, I often find that the length of an itinerary is one of the first things I consider. It doesn't matter how amazing a trip looks if I only have 4 days and the itinerary is for 12. So I thought, why not surface this information immediately so users can make informed choices.

While not fully implemented in the current prototype, the design takes into account user preferences collected during onboarding. When users first set up the app, they can specify:

  • Whether they are more interested in nearby or faraway countries
  • Whether they prefer short trips (2-5 days) or longer trips (7-10+ days)

These preferences will be used to prioritise which itineraries appear first. When users see a trip that matches both their interests and their available time, they are more likely to actually use or follow the itinerary.

Since many content creators travel across multiple regions within a country (e.g. Penange -> Kuala Lumpur -> Johor Bahru), I included a mini text indicator to show this movement. However due to space contraints, only the first and last locations are shown (e.g. Penang -> ... -> Johor Bahru)

This design serves two purposes:

  • It lets users know the itinerary covers more than one location, encouraging deeper exploration
  • It maintains clean UI spacing, avoiding overwhelming text clutter

View detailed activities

If users choose to filter itineraries based on specific preferences like sightseeing, food, culture, or nature, users will be presented with a refined list of activities relevant to their interests. Even when hundreds of users have documented the same place, the activities will be grouped by location tag. Clicking into any of them brings the user to a detailed view page.

On the listing page, I have included a small but powerful text indicator showing how many itineraries that activity appears in.

"In 5,189 itineraries"

Travelers have different trip planning styles. For some people, they prefer to hit the most popular, must-see spots. Including the number of times a place appears in real itineraries helps users to:

  • Quickly identify high-interest destinations
  • Prioritise "can't-miss" attraactions without needing to read every review
  • Feel more confident about where they are going ("if 5,000+ people added this, it must be worth seeing!")

This is a form of organic social proof that is not based on ratings or ads, but actual travel behaviour.

Each detail page is enriched with photos uploaded by the creator during their trip. These images serve not just as visual documentation, but also as a powerful emotional drive to engage and inspire users.

However, I know that not all images are equally helpful.

Selfies or blurry food shots might not give a true sense of the place, and in many cases, it might affect the first impression of a destination. To ensure that the most useful and engaging visuals are surfaced, I propose using AI or image-detection to dynamically select an image to be used as a thumbnail or prioritise posts by creators that are more visually engaging.

Now you might be thinking... isn't this just a mini Google review?

One of the core goals of this app is to be a centralised, end-to-end travel companion. If users constantly have to jump between this app and external platforms like Google, it breaks the experience.

Sometimes, Google reviews often feel like reading a novel or a space for unsatisfied customers to leave grandmother stories. In this app, the review (or note) format is intentionally short and structured. Finally, reviews aren't just for browsing, they are directly tied to action.

  • There's a word count limit to keep it clear and digestible
  • Notes are meant to highlight essentials (e.g. "go early", "not worth the queue", "great for kids"
  • If a user likes what they see, they can add the activity immediately to their own itinerary

Think of it as micro-reviews tailored for travelers, not a public comment section.

When a user saves an activity (e.g., Senso-ji Temple) into their own itinerary, it is added as a blank entry. This entry only include the location and basic data. This allows users to come back and fill in the details later, upload their own photos, add notes and other context when their trip has ended.

Why this matters?

Sometimes, users are activitly on their trip or planning spontaneously. Giving them the flexibility to log ideas quickly without filling everything out makes the app feel light, fast and travel-friendly.

View profile

Like all social media platforms, profile viewing is a crucial feature that enhances user interaction. On the left side, we display the profile view of a creator, where they can access itineraries that are still in 'draft' mode. This allows creators to manage and refine their itineraries before sharing them with the public.

On the right side, public viewers can see itineraries once they are published. At this stage, they have the option to bookmark their favorite itineraries for easy access later. This bookmarking feature encourages engagement, as users can save itineraries they find appealing, allowing them to revisit and plan their trips based on the curated experiences of others.

Overall, this profile view fosters a sense of community and collaboration, making it easier for users to share and discover travel plans.

I believe that personal insights can be just as valuable in shaping an app's functionality and user experience

I truly enjoyed working on this project. It was a fulfilling experience that allowed me to channel my creativity and passion for travel into something tangible. Although this version may not fully represent my comprehensive 'user research,' I wanted to design based on my own values and preferences for this project.

It's important to note that this app is far from a final product, and there is still a wealth of features and enhancements that could significantly boost its overall value. I have plenty of ideas for future updates and functionalities that could further enrich the user experience and broaden its appeal.
