Intranet Portal Revamp: Improving Usability and Design

This project was a revamp of an intranet portal used by employees to access workplace information. The portal was being migrated to a newer version of SharePoint, presenting an opportunity to improve both its usability and visual design.

15 mins read
Back to my work

Overview

Role: Team Lead, UI/UX Designer

Responsibilities: Managed project timelines and milestone, facilitated workshops and user sessions, contributed directly to both UI design (SharePoint theming, templates) and UX (IA restructuring, testing, analysis) and acted as the primary liaison with the client's steering committee

Team: Managed a 3-person team, including a dedicated UI designer and a dedicated UX researcher

Duration: 7 months

Platform: SharePoint Migration (SharePoint 2016 to SharePoint Subscription Edition)

About the project

For confidentiality reasons, the client's name will not be mentioned in this portfolio.

The intranet portal was cluttered and difficult for staff to navigate, making information hard to find. The migration to a newer version of SharePoint created an opportunity to not only refresh the design but also restructure the information architecture.

The Goals

1. Information Architecture (IA) and Wayfinding: Reorganise and simplify the intranet's structure to make information easier to find, tested and validated through card sorting and tree testing with employees across six departments

2. Page layouts and templates: Develop a set of reusable content templates that allow content owners to easily publish information without manually configuring layouts or widgets, ensuring efficiency and consistency

3. Interface refresh: Update the portal's interface to align with the latest SharePoint theme, giving it a modern, user-friendly look that improves the overall employee experience.

To achieve the goals, we carried out five focused activities

01

Hybrid card sorting activity

Due to the time constraints and large user base, we decided with the client to conduct a mass card sorting activity instead of holding smaller separate sessions. The session was conducted over 2 days. Each day consists of 5 tables with 6 participants per table.

  • 60 employees in total, representing multiple departments, split into two groups
  • New employees (0-2 years) - less familiar with internal jargon and navigation
  • Senior employees (2+ years) - more experienced and accustomed to finding information

The current information architecture

Please note that this information architecture is a simplified version for easy reference and does not fully represent the site's actual structure. It is meant to help you understand my explanation.
(Example of the current information architecture)

The findings

1. Current policies and guidelines are in separate folders from the actual service. Employees must navigate multiple times to gather the information they need before submitting a claim, resulting in excessive clicks and a less efficient experience.

"You cannot expect me to find information in one place, then do things in another place. The intent has to be packaged"

2. Employees often struggle to distinguish between policies and guidelines, and may end up checking both folders to find the relevant information.

"If the header has the word 'policy', then I will know it's a policy, or else I wouldn't know"

In summary, there are employees who would like to merge everything together so that it's easier for them to find information.

02

Hybrid tree-testing

For the tree-testing tasks, we made a few changes to the information architecture, such as relabeling and resturcturing. While we uncovered several findings, I'll focus on the key one, which is to merge services with policies and guidelines under a single umbrella, rather than keeping them separate.

(Example of the information architecture based on card-sorting activity)

We decided to run a split tree test to compare.

  • We sent 50 participants an unmoderated link for Tree 1, which reflects the current design with services separate from policies and guidelines
  • And another 50 participants an unmoderated link for Tree 2, reflecting the merger of services and policies and guidelines
  • We also conducted a moderated session with 20 participants (10 per tree) to explore their reasoning
The goal was to compare both designs and see if one performed noticeably better or revealed significant differences.

The findings

After considering task success rates, time on task, directness, and error rates, we found that Tree 1 and Tree 2 performed almost evenly. From this, we concluded:
  1. Both structures are viable: Both are understandable for users
  2. Different mindsets are at play: This reflects the different mental models within the organisation
  3. Tree testing has limitaions: Because it isolates navigation from the actual UI, it doesn't capture the influence of labels, layout, or visual hierarchy

What we did next

To better understand the "why" behind participants' choices, here's what we uncovered from our moderated sessions:

Tree 1 supporters (separate Services and P&G) are resistant to change as they worried a new structure would force them to relearn where everything is. They valued a cleaner, less cluttered experience.

"... would take some time and effort to get used to"

"info that I'm familiar with, I don't need to refer to the P&G"

Tree 2 supporters (merge Services and P&G) preferred information to be packaged together as a set. They saw value in a one-stop page, and felt it reduced trial-and-error when searching.

"Everything is searchable in one place. Save time in searching. More productivity in our work."

"One stop to learn about guidelines, then immediately follow through with executions as opposed to going to two different locations"

Our recommendation

With results nearly split, we knew the chocie would be tough for the client. Guided by best practices and Hick's Law, we recommended Tree A (separate) as the safer option and created a mock-up to help them visualise it, giving them confidence to go ahead with the proposed solution.

The image below shows the Services listing page. Services linked to policies or guidelines displays an icon. Hovering on the icon shows a tooltip, and clicking on the icon opens a popup with the relevant information. This way it keeps services separate but connected to important information without clutter.

(This mockup is for illustrative purposes only and does not reflect the final design)

Based on participants' feedback that policies and guidelines are hard to differentiate unless the headers explicitly include the words, we decided to use tagging. This makes it clearer for users while keeping headers less repetitive.

03

Homepage design co-creation with employees

The goal of the session was to collaboratively design the ideal layout for the refreshed homepage with those frequent users. As part of the session, participants were asked to sketch their desired homepage and explain their choices, allowing us to better understand their priorities and the reasoning behind their designs.

(Design example created by participants, which we then digitised)

We asked participants to present their designs and explain their reasoning, followed by group feedback to discuss agreements and differences. Afterwards, they used coloured stickers to mark elements they liked most, helping us identify common preferences and merge features into a preferred design.

The findings

From this activity, we identified key homepage elements valued by participants, such as discussion forums, announcements and quick links. They also expressed a preference for a simple homepage focused on essential information, and a clear dislike for corporate content they viewed as unnecessary or "advertisement-like", which cluttered the space.

04

Homepage design poll

Based on the feedback from Activity 03: Homepage design co-creation with employees, we created three polished design styles inspired by participants' sketches. We ran a homepage design poll through the company's internal polling system. Employees were asked to choose their preferred layout from three design options. The resulting statistics offer clear insights into employee preferences, helping to facilitate management approval of the proposed design.

With 3,500 employees, a minimum of 94 responses were needed for reliable results. Receiving 366 responses gives strong confidence in the data, supporting management's approval of the proposed design.

The homepage design concepts were:
(Design visuals are excluded since it mainly comprise SharePoint widgets)

  • Back to basics: Designed for employees who are comfortable with the current setup and prefer minimal changes
  • Refreshing: Designed for employees who want a quick snapshop of what's happening
  • Function over form: Intended for employees seeking a new experience with a clean, functional interface, offering more information for a cohesive experience

The findings

52% of employees (190 out of 366) selected Design B, Refreshing. This preference held consistent across all age groups and experience levels, where a majority in each segment also chose Design B. This indicates broad, organisation-wide support for moving forward with the Refreshing design.

05

Content templates co-creation with content owners

The goal is to understand the content creation and publishing process, including what works and what doesn't, governance and roles in creation, editing and approval. We also identified content types and templates for efficiency, drafting initial version of content owners to refine. Engagement focused on high-priority owners who manage substantial or frequently updated content.

We conducted 4 rounds of workshops across 4 departments of content creators, with a total of about 30 participants.

While SharePoint allows flexibility through drag-and-drop components, providing templates helps content creators build pages more efficiently without extensive tweaking. These templates serve as quick-start guides, streamlining the content creation process.

What I've learned

This project taught me a lot about time management as I had to run UX and UI activities in parallel while ensuring both received the attention they needed. I also gained experience in technical research and collaboration, particularly with SharePoint, where I had to understand system limitations and coordinate with the technical team to determine what was feasible and what wasn't.

Managing large-scaled workshops was another key learning point. While smaller workshops are often more effective, time constraints meant we had to work with larger groups. To make these sessions successful, I had to carefully prepare resources, train facilitators and plan activities to ensure the workshops ran smoothly.
