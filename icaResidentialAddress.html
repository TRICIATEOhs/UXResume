ICA - Update Residential Address

As part of ICA’s ongoing efforts to digitalise their services, this new e-Service is available since 1 October 2020. All Singapore Residents (Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents) who need to report a change of residential address can do so online.

Overview

Role: Main UI/UX Designer

Responsibilities: Conducted usability testing with 15 public users, analysed findings and contributed directly to UI redesign and overall user experience improvements

Duration: 3 months

Platform: Conducted remote usability testing via UXArmy and Google Hangouts during COVID

The original prototype was designed by a former colleague who has since left the organisation. I assumed responsibility for conducting Usability Testing and improved the design based on feedback gathered from participants.

Change of residential address no longer requires police station visit

Before this e-Service, Singaporean residents had to update their address in person at the police station, presenting their IC and proof of new address.

When I first heard about digitising this process, I was skeptical, especially since government services serve a wide range of users, some of whom prefer face-to-face interaction. I focused on designing with empathy, ensuring the digital process was efficient, intuitive, and supportive for users of all tech comfort levels.

New workflow

Residents will submit a form indicating their new residential address
A mail containing a unique PIN will be mailed to the new address
Residents need the letter to verify the new address with the PIN
Residents will login to the ICA e-Service and enter the verification PIN
Residents will receive stickers for their IC's new address

We recruited a total of 15 participants, strategically spread across three distinct age groups

1. Age 20–39: Young working adults

This group represents the most tech-savvy demographic. They are generally comfortable navigating digital interfaces and often expect efficiency and speed in online services.

2. Age 40-59: Middle-aged adults

While still relatively tech-proficient, users in this group may have varying degrees of digital comfort. Their feedback is valuable in identifying areas where clarity could be improved, especially for services that might be used infrequently.

3. Age 60-70: Older adults

We set a minimum digital literacy requirement, but this group could still struggle with complex interactions. Their participant was key in identifying pain points and ensuring the service remained approachable and supportive for less tech-savvy users.

Usability testing was conducted online due to COVID-19 restrictions

Due to Covid-19, all usability testing was conducted via UXArmy, with Google Hangout used for real-time communication. Participants shared their screens while completing tasks, allowing us to capture video and audio, though body cues were not visible.

Before the usability testing, four sets of tasks was prepared for evaluation:

Task 1: Change Your Residential Address

“You have recently moved into a new apartment with your spouse. Perform the action to change your residential address to the new place”

Task 2: Receive a New Verification PIN

“You have waited for 5 days after your application and you have not received the verification PIN as mentioned. Perform the action to get your verification PIN”

The aim of this task is to evaluate a new feature for requesting a new verification PIN. I needed to observe how participants would navigate through the process to accomplish this task.

Task 3: Complete Your Application Process

“You have successfully received a new mail after requesting for a new PIN. Perform the action to complete your application process.”

I aimed to ascertain whether participants understood how to use the verification PIN.

Task 4: Change Residential Address for Others

“After you’ve shifted out with your spouse, your elderly parents (who are not tech-savvy) decided to downgrade their flat to a smaller unit. Perform the action to change the residential address on their behalf.”

Since this is a newly introduced feature, I sought to evaluate participants' ability to complete the task and gather any questions or concerns they may have regarding the feature.

Through evaluating the design in its early stage, here's what stood out to me

01

e-Services listing page

Upon logging into the e-Service, users will arrive at this listing page where they can choose the transaction they wish to complete.

The findings
  • Participants were unsure of what to do upon reaching the listing page and remained stuck there for a while
  • Participants created a “mental checklist” of what they have already done as there are no indication of their next step (Okay, I’ve already done this… I’ve done that too… I think I need to do this now…)

02

Persona selection

When clicking on any transaction listed above, users will be directed to a "Persona Selection" page where they must specify whether they are completing the transaction for themselves, for others, or for themselves and their family members.

The findings
  • Participants interpreted 'Others' differently. Some saw it as only blood relatives, while others saw it as only their spouse
  • Certain participants expressed skepticism regarding conducting a transaction on behalf of 'Others', feeling uneasy about the possibility of altering the residential address for an unfamiliar individual

Why is this feature required?

Like many users, I also felt that it was an unusual feature that could be easily misunderstood and whether it might open doors to misuse (updating someone else's address without proper concent).

To address the potential for misuse, the client clarified these built-in safeguards:

• Singpass authentication
Anyone performing this action must be logged in with their own Singpass account, which provdes a strong digital identity trail. All backend activitiy is logged and traceable, serving as a deterrent for foul play

• Sensitive data requirement
In order to update someone else's address, the individual must possess specific personal identification details of the person they're helping. These are not easily accessible and typically require trust and permission, serving as an additional layer of protection

03

Form submission

If participants choose "For Myself and Others," the "Particulars of Applications" section will provide them with the option to add any number of additional applicants (family members residing with the main applicant) using the "Add Applicant" button.

The findings
  • I've noticed that some participants overlooked adding the second applicant and attempted to submit the form
  • Therefore, participants suggested that displaying the empty fields for the second person's particulars upfront would simplify the process and make the system more foolproof

04

Request for new PIN

After submitting the form, residents must await mail delivery within 3-5 working days. Yet, there might be instances where residents do not receive their mail. Thus, we devised Task 2 to assess this scenario. Our goal was to observe the steps participants would take in such a situation.

Once participants click on “Verify PIN” on the listing page above, they will be directed to the following screen. Only on this page will they see the option to request a new verification PIN.

The findings

This task experienced the highest rate of failure.
  • Participants didn't think of clicking “Verify PIN” because doing so implies they already have the PIN to verify. Since participants didn't receive the PIN, clicking on that option didn't seem logical
  • As such, some participants were looking for some kind helpline or chatbot to ask directly

Participant interactions were analysed through recorded sessions, focusing on clicking patterns and verbal feedback

After reviewing the recorded clips of all 15 participants, I noted their clicking behaviors, verbal cues, and potential opportunities to enhance the e-Service's user-friendliness. This enabled me to identify similar behavioral patterns and areas for improvement that may have been overlooked.

Subsequently, I consolidated my findings into a presentation slide and shared them with the stakeholders.

The scoring metrics used in this usability study were also guided by the principles outlined in Nielsen Norman Group's article, "Success Rate: The Simplest Usability Metric"

I adopted a four-tier usability outcome model inspired by their approach but tailored to this study's context.

1. Direct success
Users complete the task exactly as expected, following the designated path without any detours

2. Indirect success
Users still complete the task, but take a roundabout route, possibly due to mis-clicks, backtracking or side exploration

3. Indirect failure (or assisted failure)
Users eventually fail or miss the task but only after receiving help or hints. It's like partial success, but not an independent one.

4. Direct failure
Users fail to complete the task or abandon it without reaching the intended destination, showing clear roadblocks.

The design was reassessed and enhanced as a result of the findings

I was tasked with conducting only one round of Usability Testing. While there wasn't a formal evaluation session to assess the success of my updated design, I personally reached out to a few close friends and family members to gather their feedback.

01

The landing page was redesigned into a step-by-step guided layout consisting of three clear stages

The original landing page consisted of a simple table listing available features. However, user testing revealed confusion around what action to take next and how to navigate the process. Many users were unsure where to begin, leading to hesitation and frustration.

To address this, I redesigned the landing page into a step-by-step guided layout consisting of three clear stages. This approach offers several key improvements:

• Visual clarity through progression
The step-by-step structure helps reduce users' cognitive load by clearing showing where they are in the process, what they have completed, and what comes next.

• Enhanced visual cues
I incorporated illustrative icons to create stronger visual associations and aid users in understanding each step at a glace. This also made the interface feel more approachable and informative.

• Improved information flow
By breaking down the process into bite-sized steps, users are less overwhelmed and can better comprehend the overall workflow of the e-Service

• Focused call-to-action
The primary action, "Start", is visually emphasised, while secondary or non-critical actions are dimmed to avoid distraction. This ensures that users are guided toward the correct first step with minimal ambiguity.

02

Use of icons in persona selection page to enhance both usability and visual clarity

The original page used basic radio buttons with text-only labels to let users choose who they were submitting the address change for. While functional, the layout felt visually bare due to excessive white space, and the use of plain radio buttons lacked intuitive context.

To enhance both usability and visual clarity, I redesigned the page with the following updates:

• Icon-based selection for better recognition
I replaced standard radio buttons with illustrative icons representing each persona type. This allowed users to make quicker associations. I specifically choen an elderly couple icon for "Others" as this option most often applies to elderly parents, or individual who require assistance from family or social workers

The icons also helped balance the layout by reducing visual emptiness and giving the screen more structure and personality.

• Contextual tooltip for "Others"
During earlier testing and review, the term "Others" caused confusion as some user interpreted it as if they could update the address on behalf of neighbours, friends, or even random strangers (as long as they have their personal details).

To address this, I added a tooltip next to the "Others" label, clearly explaining that it refers to dependents such as elderly parents, or those unable to access the service themselves. This is to help remove ambiguity and make sure the option is used correctly.

03

Anticipated real-world scenarios beyond requirements

While brainstorming the 3-step process for the address change flow, I also consider edge cases and real-life user concerns that were not initially covered but could significantly impact the user experience.

One of the first questions I asked myself was:

"What if someone accidentally submitted the wrong address, like entering Blk 187 instead of Blk 178?"

This led me to think about additional user needs and possible follow-up scenarios, such as:

  • How can a user amend a mistake after submission?
  • What if the user doesn't receive the address label (similar to missing verification PIN)
  • How do they apply the sticker correctly to their IC?

As a result, I proposed the inclusion of contextual help and supporting guidance on the landing page. This allows users to feel more informed and supported throughout the entire process.

Among the various age groups, I noted an interesting observation

Participants aged between their 20s and 40s actively seek information, whereas those in their 50s and 60s prefer information to be straightforward and obvious.

The younger participants were comfortable navigating the site, using back buttons to return to previous pages.

However, the older participants were hesitant to explore, fearing they might make mistakes or be unable to undo them. Therefore, my updated design aims to alleviate cognitive burden on users.
